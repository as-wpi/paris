# Reading (written after the run; rule frozen before it)

**All six arms RETIRE.** None reaches even the ~1-point switch-side ceiling that test A measured, let alone the 2-point bar.

**E, circuit breaker: inert.** At −2σ and −3σ on the 21-day return the breaker almost never fires while the switch is still on — by the time a 21-day loss reaches two sigmas of the incumbent's own volatility scale, the jump model has usually already flipped off. Every metric is identical to the incumbent to three decimals. The one place it acts, 1995–99, it cuts a bull-market drawdown by a point at no cost, which is not what it was built for.

**F, asymmetric penalty: the only arm that changes the crashes, and it pays in Sharpe.** Cheaper exits help exactly where expected — LTCM 1998 MaxDD −5.5% and −4.7% against −7.9%, 2020 −10.3% against −11.9%, 2018-Q4 −5.5% against −6.1% — but the 1987 episode is slightly worse and the fast-crash median lands at +0.0. Over the full sample the cheaper exit costs 0.013 Sharpe at penalty 2 (CI excluding zero) and 0.028 at penalty 1, with Martin falling from 0.63 to 0.55 in both: the extra exits are mostly false ones. The switch's edge is its reluctance to move, and lowering the toll on one side spends that edge.

**G, turbulence halving: the nearest miss, and a different object.** Halving exposure on turbulent days improves the full-sample Martin (own 0.70, SPY-based 0.78 against 0.63) and the full-sample MaxDD (−25.4% for the SPY version) at a Sharpe cost within the bar (−0.004 and −0.010), but it does nothing for the fast-crash episodes (±0.0), raises turnover by half (×1.59, ×1.53), and the SPY version bleeds in the calm controls. What it improves is the ordinary drawdown distribution outside crashes, which is a sizing-layer property, not the crash-speed question this sequence was registered to answer; it also behaves like the rejected volatility gate under another name (turbulence is squared standardised return, a volatility measure). It is reported as the result it is and not carried forward under this pre-registration.

**Where the crash-speed question stands after A–G.** The attribution bound said 41% of fast-crash drawdown was reachable with five days of foreknowledge, about half from the sizer and less from the switch. Every causal attempt to buy a piece of that — four estimators, two fast decays, a jump rule, two breakers, two asymmetric tolls, two turbulence caps — either reached a fraction of its ceiling at a Sharpe or bleed cost, or did nothing. The consistent reason is that the first two or three days of a crash are informationally silent to any daily close-to-close filter, and the days after that are already handled by the incumbent. Only H, the drawdown governor, remains: a rule on the P&L rather than a forecast, which sidesteps the information problem and pays a known structural price instead.
